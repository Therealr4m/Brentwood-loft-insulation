Is Rigid PIR Insulation Better Than Mineral Wool?

One of the most common questions we get from homeowners in Brentwood is whether rigid PIR insulation is actually better than mineral wool. The honest answer is that it depends entirely on the loft, the available space and what you're trying to achieve.

When comparing PIR vs mineral wool, many people assume PIR must automatically be the better option because it offers a higher thermal performance per millimetre. While that is true, mineral wool remains the most commonly installed loft insulation across Brentwood and throughout the UK for good reason.

Over the years we've worked on loft insulation projects ranging from older CM13 semis with outdated insulation to modern properties where space restrictions made rigid PIR boards the more practical solution. Both materials have advantages and both have situations where they perform best.

What Is Mineral Wool Loft Insulation?

Mineral wool is the traditional loft insulation material found in most UK homes. It is typically supplied in rolls and installed across the loft floor between and over ceiling joists.

Modern mineral wool products provide excellent thermal performance when installed to the recommended depth. For most Brentwood properties, this means building up insulation to approximately 270mm.

One reason mineral wool remains popular is because it is cost-effective, relatively straightforward to install and works exceptionally well in standard loft spaces where there is sufficient depth available.

It is also flexible, allowing installers to work around pipework, cables and awkward loft layouts without leaving significant gaps.

What Is Rigid PIR Loft Insulation?

Rigid PIR insulation is a foam board insulation product often faced with foil on both sides. Unlike mineral wool, PIR is supplied as solid boards rather than rolls.

The main advantage of PIR insulation is its thermal efficiency. It can achieve similar insulation values to much thicker layers of mineral wool while taking up significantly less space.

This makes it particularly useful in loft conversions, room-in-roof properties and loft spaces where headroom is limited.

In Brentwood, we often see PIR used around loft hatches, storage areas, dormer sections and roof slopes where maintaining usable space is important.

PIR vs Mineral Wool: Thermal Performance

If we're looking purely at insulation performance per millimetre, PIR insulation generally wins.

A relatively thin PIR board can provide similar thermal resistance to a much thicker layer of mineral wool. This is why PIR is frequently chosen where space restrictions exist.

However, thermal performance isn't the only factor that matters.

Most traditional lofts in Brentwood have plenty of room above the ceiling joists. In those situations, adding mineral wool to the recommended depth often delivers excellent results without the additional material cost associated with PIR boards.

For a standard loft floor insulation project, homeowners are often surprised to learn that mineral wool remains the more practical solution despite PIR's higher thermal rating.

Which Is Better For Loft Insulation In Brentwood?

For the majority of standard lofts, mineral wool is usually the material we recommend first.

  • Cost-effective for large loft areas
  • Easy to install at 270mm depth
  • Excellent thermal performance when correctly fitted
  • Suitable for most traditional roof spaces
  • Widely used throughout the UK

Rigid PIR tends to become the better option when space is limited or where specific parts of the loft require higher thermal performance without adding significant thickness.

  • Loft conversions
  • Sloping roof sections
  • Dormer walls
  • Restricted attic spaces
  • Storage areas requiring raised flooring systems

This is why many projects actually use both materials rather than treating PIR vs mineral wool as an either-or decision.

Cost Comparison: PIR vs Mineral Wool

From a material cost perspective, mineral wool is usually considerably cheaper.

For homeowners simply looking to improve energy efficiency and reduce heat loss through the loft floor, mineral wool often provides the strongest value for money.

PIR boards are generally more expensive to purchase and can require additional cutting and fitting time during installation.

That doesn't mean PIR is poor value. If limited space prevents sufficient mineral wool depth from being installed, PIR may deliver better overall results despite the higher initial cost.

The best choice depends on the layout of the property rather than simply choosing the cheapest or most expensive material available.

What We Commonly See In Brentwood Properties

Many older Brentwood homes still have insulation depths well below current recommendations. In these properties, mineral wool top-ups often provide the biggest improvement for the least cost.

We've worked on numerous properties where existing insulation was sitting at around 100mm to 150mm. Simply bringing the insulation depth up to modern standards significantly improved heat retention without the need for PIR boards throughout the entire loft.

On the other hand, some newer properties and converted loft spaces have limited room available. In these situations, rigid PIR insulation frequently becomes part of the solution because it achieves strong thermal performance while preserving usable space.

The right approach comes from assessing the loft properly rather than selecting a material based purely on marketing claims.

Final Verdict: Is Rigid PIR Better Than Mineral Wool?

If you're comparing PIR vs mineral wool purely on thermal performance per millimetre, rigid PIR insulation is generally superior.

However, for most standard loft insulation projects in Brentwood, mineral wool remains the most practical and cost-effective choice. It performs extremely well when installed to the correct depth and continues to be the material used in the majority of loft insulation upgrades.

PIR insulation becomes particularly valuable where space is restricted, in loft conversions or in areas where greater thermal performance is needed without adding significant thickness.

The best solution is often determined by the property itself rather than declaring one material universally better than the other.
